A typical tank water heater gives eight to twelve years of service in Chicago, IL, and that number drops with hard water, high pressure, or a tank nobody ever flushed. Most tanks give a few warnings near the end: water seeping from under the jacket, rusty hot water, rumbling that grows louder every month, or a supply that runs out sooner each winter. A leak from the tank body is the clearest signal of all. When the glass lining gives out and the steel corrodes through, there is no part to replace, and a slow weep can turn into a flooded floor with no notice.

Choosing the replacement is where most of the value is. The old heater's size is only a starting point, since families grow, bathrooms get added, and a 40-gallon tank that was fine a decade ago may now run out every morning. We size tanks by first-hour rating and tankless units by gallons per minute at your winter temperature rise, and we explain what each option costs up front and month to month. The simplest, least expensive path is usually a like-for-like gas or electric tank. Tankless and heat pump models carry a higher price but can trim operating costs, and they come with their own requirements for gas line size, venting, electrical capacity, or room to breathe. We lay out the facts, then you decide.

Installation day runs on a checklist: the old tank drained and hauled away, new connectors and a working shutoff installed, gas lines leak-tested and fitted with a sediment trap, venting sized and pitched to the manufacturer's spec, and the relief valve piped to a safe discharge point. If local code requires a thermal expansion tank, a drain pan, or seismic straps, they go in, and the permit is pulled whenever one is needed. We finish by filling and purging the tank, checking draft and carbon monoxide on gas models, setting the thermostat near 120 degrees, and showing you around the new heater. Warning Signs to Watch For

Catching a symptom early is often the difference between a quick part swap and a tank that gives out on the garage floor. Keep an eye out for the following:

  • Energy bills rising while hot water gets harder to come by
  • Repeated service calls on the same aging heater
  • Discolored hot water, a sign the tank is corroding inside
  • Visible corrosion around the pipe connections or burner area
  • A household that has outgrown the tank's capacity
  • Water leaking from the bottom or body of the tank
  • Loud rumbling or popping that flushing no longer fixes
  • A tank at or past the eight-to-twelve-year mark

Why Hire a Professional for Water Heater Replacement?

Start with the venting. A gas water heater's exhaust contains carbon monoxide, and a vent that is undersized, poorly pitched, or loosely connected can let it spill back into your home. Add a gas connection that has to be leak-free, an electrical circuit that has to match the new heater's load, and a relief valve that has to carry the right rating with a discharge line that is never capped or undersized, and there is a lot to get right. Many homes also need an expansion tank, a drain pan, or seismic straps under local code, and some jurisdictions require a permit. Switching to tankless or a heat pump adds new gas line sizing, vent materials, circuits, and condensate lines to the list.

A professional replacement covers the whole installation, not just the tank. Sizing is done by first-hour rating or flow rate. Fuel, venting, and electrical capacity are verified, and whatever expansion tank, drain pan, or straps your home needs get added. We leak-test every joint, check draft and carbon monoxide on gas units, and haul the old heater off. The result is a heater that is safe, sized for your household, and ready for inspection if one is required. If your current tank still has good years left and just needs a part, we will tell you directly.

Water Heater Care Tips From Our Technicians

Routine maintenance is almost always cheaper and easier than an early replacement. Follow these tips from our technicians:

  • Inspect the anode rod every few years; replacing it can add years to the tank
  • Heat pump units need a clean filter and open airflow to run efficiently
  • Flush the new tank once or twice a year to keep sediment from building up
  • Have water pressure checked; above about 80 psi, a pressure reducing valve protects the new heater
  • Write the install date on the tank so you know when to plan the next replacement
  • Tankless units need a yearly descaling, especially where the water is hard
  • Keep the temperature near 120 degrees for safety and slower mineral buildup
