An electric water heater does not burn anything, so it is tempting to think nothing much can go wrong. In practice, the parts that do the work (two heating elements, two thermostats, and a high-limit safety switch) fail in predictable ways. The upper thermostat heats the top third of the tank first, then switches power to the lower element. If the upper element or thermostat fails, the lower one never gets its turn, and you get no hot water at all. If the lower element burns out, the top of the tank still heats, so you get a short burst of hot water that runs out fast. For Chicago, IL homeowners, knowing which symptom you have is the first step toward the right fix.

Electric water heaters are straightforward to test with the right meter and training, and that is exactly how Chicago Water Heater starts. At each Chicago home, our technicians measure element resistance, check for elements shorted to the tank, test thermostat operation, confirm the breaker and supply voltage, and inspect every connection from the junction box to the terminals. We also look at sediment. A lower element buried in mineral buildup runs hot, fails early, and can make the tank noisy, so whenever we pull an element, we check how much sediment is sitting on the bottom and flush it out if needed.

Once we know what failed, the fix is usually quick. Elements, thermostats, and high-limit switches are standard parts, and our trucks carry the common sizes. Replacing an element means shutting off power, draining the tank, installing the new element, and refilling completely before power is restored, which protects the new element from a dry fire. We also repair damaged wiring, tighten or replace terminal connections, and flush sediment from the tank bottom. If replacement makes more sense, we install new electric heaters sized to your household's first-hour demand, confirm the breaker and wire gauge are correct, and add the expansion tank, drain pan, and strapping your local code requires. Warning Signs to Watch For

Catching a symptom early is often the difference between a quick part swap and a tank that gives out on the garage floor. If any of the following sound familiar, it's worth having the unit checked:

  • No hot water at all from any faucet
  • A shower that turns lukewarm much sooner than it used to
  • The water heater breaker tripping over and over
  • The high-limit reset popping again after you push it
  • Rust-colored hot water or moisture around the heater
  • Browned wiring or a burnt odor from behind the access cover
  • Dangerously hot water, a sign a thermostat may be stuck
  • A hissing or sizzling noise near the lower element

Why Professional Electric Water Heater Services Matters

Two things make electric water heater repair trickier than it looks: the power involved and the overlap between symptoms. Every element and thermostat connects to 240-volt terminals, and those terminals stay live until the breaker is off and the circuit is tested dead. On the diagnostic side, a heater that will not heat might have a failed element, a bad thermostat, a tripped reset, or a wiring problem, and a reset that keeps tripping is a warning, not an annoyance. Guessing gets expensive fast.

What a trained technician brings is certainty. We test instead of guessing, from element resistance and ground-fault checks to thermostat operation and supply voltage. We replace wiring that heat has damaged, tighten or replace terminal connections, and refill the tank completely before power goes on, so a new element is never destroyed by a dry fire. And because some electric heaters are simply worn out, we give you a straight answer on repair versus replacement, including whether a heat pump water heater might make sense for your home.

Water Heater Care Tips From Our Technicians

Most of the failures we see could have been delayed for years with basic upkeep. Start with these practical measures our team relies on:

  • Treat a tripped high-limit as a warning sign, not a nuisance
  • Flush sediment from the tank once or twice a year so it cannot bury the lower element
  • Lift the T&P valve lever yearly to make sure it opens and reseats
  • Replace a worn anode rod before corrosion moves on to the tank itself
  • After any draining, refill the tank fully and run a hot faucet before restoring power
  • Consider a water softener if you have hard water, since it slows sediment and scale on the elements
  • Choose a setting of about 120 degrees to balance comfort, safety, and operating cost
